Kindly note: the most expensive silence in Indian aviation last year did not happen at a boarding gate. It happened inside the company.

In the first week of December 2025, IndiGo, the airline that taught a nation to expect its flight on time, cancelled flights by the thousand. Passengers got an apology, refunds and vouchers. All of that was announced in public, and all of it was needed. What the public never saw was a single honest sentence addressed to the people actually running the airline. This case file is about that sentence, and the cost of not writing it.

## The Snapshot

What happened: between 2 and 9 December, India's largest airline watched its crew rosters collapse under new rest rules for pilots.

What it said: sorry to customers within three days. The real cause, however, was named first by its pilots and then by the regulator, before the airline got round to it.

The verdict: customer recovery, adequate. Internal communication, absent. The crew's story reached the country through a pilots' federation, a minister and a news agency. Good apology to the passengers: poor conversation with the people.

## Trigger and Context

The rule was not a surprise. The DGCA's revised Flight Duty Time Limitation norms gave pilots more rest and fewer night landings. Airlines had a long run-up. The Federation of Indian Pilots put it at a two-year preparatory window.

The warning was not a surprise either. IndiGo cancelled 951 flights in November, a figure the DGCA later cited when it doubled the airline's schedule cut.

Then the floor gave way. On-time performance across 1 to 5 December fell to 3.7 per cent. For an airline whose entire brand is the word "on time", that is not a bad week. That is a bad identity.

The DGCA's inquiry counted 2,507 cancellations and 1,852 delays between 3 and 5 December alone, and more than three lakh stranded passengers. Its finding, in the regulator's own words, was "over-optimisation of operations", with rosters "designed to maximise duty periods" and "minimal recovery margins".

Translated from regulator into Hindi, the roster ran on a single principle.

॥ "Sab theek ho jayega." It will all work out. It did not.

## Who Needed to Hear What

Every crisis has an audience map, whether or not anyone draws it. Here is IndiGo's, with what each audience needed and what it got.

A/. Passengers needed to know whether to leave for the airport. They got a five-cause explanation on 3 December, an apology on 5 December and vouchers on 11 December.

B/. Pilots and cabin crew needed to know whether leadership blamed them, and what the plan for rosters was. They got public silence on the cause, and an allowance revision by internal circular on 30 December.

C/. Ground and airport staff needed one thing: what to say to the furious passenger in front of them. There is no public trace of a script. There is a CEO thank-you video, on 18 December.

D/. The regulator needed a root cause and a believable recovery date. It got a request to suspend the new rules for the airline's A320 fleet until 10 February 2026.

E/. The media needed an owner and a cause. When the airline did not supply one clearly, the minister did.

F/. Investors needed to know whether this was weather or structure. They got a board-level Crisis Management Group, reported on 7 December.

Six audiences. One got a sustained, scripted response. Kindly guess which.

## In Their Own Words

What follows is quoted directly, in short excerpts, from statements as carried by the national press. No paraphrase, no tidying.

On 3 December, the airline's spokesperson explained the trouble:

॥ "A multitude of unforeseen operational challenges including minor technology glitches, schedule changes linked to the winter season, adverse weather conditions, increased congestion in the aviation system and the implementation of updated crew rostering rules (Flight Duty Time Limitations) had a negative compounding impact on our operations." IndiGo spokesperson, 3 December 2025, via Business Today.

Count them: glitches, winter, weather, congestion, and finally, in the last seat of the last row, the crew rules. Who could have guessed that the cause everyone was talking about would board last? Only everyone who has ever read a corporate statement.

On 5 December, the apology arrived on X:

॥ "We extend a heartfelt apology to all our customers and industry stakeholders who have been impacted by these events." IndiGo on X, 5 December 2025, via Deccan Herald.

The same day, CEO Pieter Elbers appeared on video and said the most honest thing anyone at the airline said all month:

॥ "Regrettably, earlier measures of the last few days have proven not to be enough." Pieter Elbers, 5 December 2025, via The Tribune.

Take a bow for that one. Plain words, no weather in sight.

On 11 December, the money:

॥ "We will offer travel vouchers worth Rs 10,000 to such severely impacted customers." IndiGo, 11 December 2025, via Deccan Herald.

And on 18 December, to employees, at last:

॥ "The worst is behind us." Pieter Elbers to staff, 18 December 2025, via Business Standard.

Now the voices that filled the gap. On 4 December, the pilots' federation did not bother with five causes. It named one:

॥ "The current disruption is the direct consequence of IndiGo's prolonged and unorthodox lean manpower strategy across departments, particularly in flight operations." Federation of Indian Pilots, 4 December 2025, via Business Today.

And on 9 December, the Civil Aviation Minister settled the matter for the nation:

॥ "IndiGo is solely responsible for the recent air crisis and has failed in its duties." K. Ram Mohan Naidu, 9 December 2025, via All India Radio News.

One caveat, issued in fairness. Every quote above is the speaker's own, but where a report summarised a speech in its own words, I left it out. A teardown that complains about loose communication should not commit any.

## The Timeline: Events Versus Words

Line up what happened against what was said, and a pattern appears.

A/. November: 951 cancellations in the month. Communication: none that the public saw.

B/. 2 and 3 December: disruption spreads across hubs. The spokesperson lists five causes.

C/. 4 December: the DGCA summons the airline. The pilots' federation blames lean manpower. The airline does not answer that point on the record.

D/. 5 December: the worst day, with more than a thousand cancellations. The apology on X, the CEO's video and its "reboot" of systems and schedules, and a request to suspend the new rules until February.

E/. 6 and 7 December: the government orders refunds and caps fares. Rs 610 crore is refunded and 3,000 bags delivered. The board forms a crisis group.

F/. 9 and 10 December: the DGCA cuts the schedule by 5 per cent, then 10. The minister calls the airline "solely responsible". Counter staff in Bengaluru describe verbal abuse and stress.

G/. 11 December: Rs 10,000 vouchers, on top of statutory compensation.

H/. 18 December: the first reported message to staff.

I/. 30 December: pilot allowances revised from 1 January, made public because Reuters read the internal report.

J/. 17 January 2026: the DGCA fines the airline Rs 22.20 crore, asks for a Rs 50 crore bank guarantee, cautions the CEO and removes the head of the operations control centre from his duties.

K/. February 2026: full compliance with the rules, and a new head for operations control.

Read it once more and look at who speaks first each time. The pilots before the airline. The minister before the airline. Reuters before the airline's own staff. In cricket terms, IndiGo spent December fielding at third man: always running after a ball someone else had hit.

## What Worked, and What Did Not

Credit first, because it is deserved.

A/. The CEO showed up on the worst day and admitted the earlier fixes had failed. Most leaders send a statement. He sent his face. Outstanding.

B/. The money moved. Rs 610 crore in refunds within days, and vouchers that came on top of statutory compensation rather than replacing it.

C/. It gave dates: normal operations between 10 and 15 December, full compliance by 10 February. Dates are a gift to every audience, because they can be checked. The airline broadly met them.

Now the bill.

A/. The first explanation diluted the cause. Five reasons for one problem does not sound thorough. It sounds like a search for company.

B/. The pilots were never answered in public. When the federation's charge goes unanswered, the minister's verdict becomes the headline. Silence is also a statement: just not one you get to write.

C/. The staff were thanked late. The first reported message to employees came on 18 December, sixteen days in, and eight days after the press reported counter staff being abused.

## The Gap

Here is the single biggest miss, and it is the reason this case file exists.

IndiGo treated December as a customer crisis. It was a workforce crisis that customers happened to see.

The cause sat inside the company: rosters with minimal recovery margins, built for the very pilots who had to fly them. Yet the crew's version of events reached the public through a federation statement, and the airline's fix for its crew, better allowances, reached the public through a news agency reading an internal circular.

Let us be fair on one point, because the record demands it. Public reporting does not show pilots demanding more money during the crisis. The revision came from the airline. The problem is not that crew asked for it. The problem is the order of events. First a structural failure, then silence, then a revised rate card. A pay change without a conversation reads like a company paying to close a story, rather than admitting that its roster design failed the people who work it. Good for the payslip: bad for the trust.

And three smaller gaps, for completeness.

A/. No frontline script. Three lakh stranded passengers met ground staff who, as far as the public record shows, had no agreed words, no clear menu of what to offer, and no stated backing when abused. That week, the counter staff were the airline's real spokespeople. Nobody briefed the spokespeople.

B/. Five causes for one problem. Inside a company, a vague explanation tells crew one of two things: leadership does not know, or leadership will not say. Neither builds trust.

C/. No two-way channel. No reported town hall, no open Q&A for crew in December. The only conversation available was through unions and the press, and so that is where the conversation went.

## My Rewrite

This is the section where the wit stops, because a memo to people working through a crisis is not the place for it. Below is what I would have sent. These are my drafts, not IndiGo documents.

The all-staff memo, to go out on the evening of 3 December, from the CEO.

Subject: What is happening, why, and what we need from each other.

To every IndiGo colleague. You will have seen today's cancellations, and many of you have stood in front of the passengers they affected. Before anyone outside the company tells you why this is happening, I want you to hear it from me.

The main cause is our own crew planning. The new duty-time rules give our pilots more rest, which is right. Our rosters were built too tightly to absorb that change, with too little room for weather or delays. That was a planning decision made at the top. It is not a failure of the people flying, serving or checking in.

What happens next. Tomorrow we will cut the schedule on purpose, so that the flights we keep actually fly: fewer cancellations announced late, more announced early. Crew rosters for the next fourteen days will be rebuilt with buffers and published by 6pm every day. Every airport team will get a one-page briefing before each shift, covering what to tell passengers, what we can offer, and whom to call when you need backup.

What I am asking of you. Tell passengers the truth, and if you do not know, say so and escalate. Look after each other: if you are abused at a counter, step away and report it, and your station manager will back you, as will I. And tell us what you see. A line for crew and frontline staff opens tonight and will be read every four hours. On Friday I will answer the most common questions on an open video call.

We built this airline on being on time. This week we were not. It is on us to fix that with you, not around you.

The frontline briefing card, one page, refreshed every shift.

A/. Say it plainly: your flight is cancelled because of our crew planning, I am sorry, here is what I can do now.

B/. Offer, in this order: the next available IndiGo seat, a full refund to the original payment method, or rebooking on another airline where possible.

C/. Never promise a time you cannot see on the system.

D/. Hand over the compensation sheet, with the statutory amount by flight time.

E/. If a passenger becomes abusive, step back and call your duty manager. You are not expected to absorb abuse.

The customer note, to go out on 4 December, a day earlier than the real apology.

We cancelled a large number of flights today, and many of you found out at the airport. That is not acceptable, and I am sorry. The cause is ours: our crew rosters did not leave enough room for new rest rules that keep our pilots safe. We are now cutting our schedule deliberately, so that we can tell you about cancellations in advance rather than at the gate. If your flight is cancelled, you can choose a free change or a full refund to your original payment method within seven days, and compensation under DGCA rules applies automatically. Please check your booking before you leave for the airport.

## How I Would Know It Worked

A rewrite without a scorecard is just nicer prose. Every measure below maps to a promise in the drafts above.

A/. Staff hear the cause from leadership first. Measure: the memo is opened within twelve hours, and a one-question pulse asks whether staff understand why flights are being cancelled. Target: 85 per cent opened, 70 per cent saying yes.

B/. A briefing before every shift. Measure: the share of airport shifts that start with the card briefed. Target: 95 per cent.

C/. Backup for abused staff. Measure: incidents reported, and how fast a duty manager responds. Target: every report acted on within thirty minutes.

D/. A two-way channel. Measure: questions received on the crew and frontline line, and the share answered within a day. Target: 90 per cent.

E/. Grievances raised inside, not outside. Measure: crew concerns raised internally versus through unions or the press, week on week. Target: the internal share rising every week.

F/. Cancellations announced early, not at the gate. Measure: the share of cancellations communicated at least 24 hours before departure. Target: rising week on week from the crisis baseline.

G/. Refunds as promised. Measure: refunds completed within seven days. Target: all of them.

H/. Trust after the storm. Measure: a January pulse asking whether leadership was honest with staff in December. Target: set the baseline, then measure it every quarter.

And the feedback loop that holds the scorecard together: read the pulse and the questions daily during the crisis and weekly after it, and answer the top three in the next staff update. People keep talking when they can see that talking changed something.

## The Monday Morning Takeaway

For anyone who runs a team, a shop or a communications desk, here is the audit. Score one point for each yes.

A/. In your last crisis, did your own people hear the cause from you before they read it somewhere else?

B/. Did the person facing the customer have words to say, or only a smile and a prayer?

C/. Was there somewhere for your staff to talk back, other than the press?

D/. Did the fix arrive with an explanation, or only on the payslip?

Zero or one: you managed a crisis. Two or three: you communicated through one. Four: kindly write the playbook, and send a copy to Gurugram.

An airline can cancel a flight and win the passenger back with a refund. It cannot cancel a conversation with its own crew and buy that back later. Wheels up, but only after the briefing.
